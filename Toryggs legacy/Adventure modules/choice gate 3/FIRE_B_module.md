SUBTITLE: Leaving Riften — Esbern's comedy re-entry

>> READ ALOUD
Dawn breaks over Riften in grey harbor-light, gulls crying over the water. You cinch your packs, count your coin twice, and load everything into the cart while Bjorn checks the wheel-pins the way he always does — twice. Mable stamps her feet in the traces, impatient to be moving. Beside her the black mare stands perfectly still, already watching the road ahead.

Klimmek crosses over while you're still finishing up and walks straight to Bjorn. He doesn't have a speech ready, and he doesn't try to fake one. "Three days I told that room the same thing," he says, "and half of them just wanted me to shut up and drink." He's grinning now. "And then you go and put together six of the best Skyrim's got, and you just — go."

He turns to the rest of you before Bjorn can answer. "All of you." His voice catches on it, and he waves the rest off with a short laugh, scrubbing a hand across his face.

Bjorn takes the offered hand in both of his instead of shaking it. He's grinning too. "You kept an old man sane the last three days, lad." He pulls Klimmek into one hard, quick embrace, claps him twice on the back, and steps away. "Go on. Get that ale finished before it goes flat."

The gates swing open. You go. Looking back once, Klimmek is still standing there, one hand raised.

The road takes you north into hills gone gold with the turn of the season. The light is thin and clean. The wheels find their rhythm, and mile by mile an old feeling settles over all of you: ease. Full packs. Paid coin. Warm bread from Riften's ovens, still soft in the saddlebags. A road that asks only that you follow it.

Mable plods on. The black mare walks beside her, head high, ears forward, reading the hills the whole way.

Then, from under the cart's floorboards — muffled by canvas and the false bottom — a dry old voice says, with enormous dignity: "...Are we far enough now? My legs fell asleep a day ago."

- If they claim it was intentional cover: he takes them at their word, and it becomes genuinely funny — he starts complimenting a plan they didn't actually have, in growing detail, until someone cracks.

**ESBERN:** "The false bottom, the silence, waiting till we were clear of the gates — very sound tradecraft." Then he keeps going. "And not one of you so much as glanced at the floor the whole ride — misdirection, in case anyone was reading your faces. Professional work." # 3 The Respite

"In the Rift, south and east of here, there is a wood called Thornheart, and its heart is alive tonight.

A sickness came into that wood once. It went down into the roots and climbed up into the heart-tree, and it made itself a body from what it stole — bark for its skin, old bone through it like veins, and two wolves of thorn and shadow that slept beneath its feet. It was patient, and it was hungry. It took three woodcutters. Then it took a priestess of Kynareth who had come to heal the trees, and it held all four of them alive inside its trunk, their faces in the wood, and it fed on them the way the sick thing feeds on the well.

Five heroes went into that wood.

The wood did not want them there. It moved its paths behind their backs. It closed the way out and unmade the way forward, and it let them wander until weaker folk would have lain down in the dark. They did not lie down. They learned the wood's own trick — that it moved when they moved, that it answered what they did — and they read it the way hunters read a trail, and it brought them, tired and bleeding, to the heart.

The heart-thing rose up to meet them, and their finest blows meant nothing. Fire broke on its bark like water. Arrows turned on the bone. The battle-mage's lance staggered it one single step — and the ground opened, and the wolves came up out of the earth, and the fight was everywhere at once.

The orc among them saw what the rest could not spare eyes for — the faces in the trunk, still living. While his companions held the beast and its wolves, he crossed that battlefield and set his blade to the wood-prison, and he cut the first woodcutter free. Then the second. And when he raised his sword to the third — the priestess — she found breath enough to beg him: wait. Careful. With the beast roaring at his back and death one heartbeat behind him, that great orc slowed his hands and cut careful, like a mother untangling a child's hair, until she came loose alive.

The beast knew what it had lost. Their lives had been its strength, and its strength was cut away. So it grew — it poured all its rage into its own body until it stood twice what it had been, and it brought its whole arm down like a felled tree on the one thing standing between it and the priestess.

He had room to run. He stood. One blow, one sound like an anvil rung once, and he was gone — and the priestess he had freed lived, because he stood.

And she — barely able to hold her own body upright — did not run either. She raised Kynareth's ward over the strangers who had come for her and gave the last light in her hands to healing them. And out of the maze came the fifth hero at last, a wizard the wood had swallowed the whole battle long, who had followed the sound of the fight through the dark, and he broke through the treeline with fire in both hands and struck the beast a blow that set it burning.

Then came the small one.

Two hands, wrapped in a fighter's old leather, and every gift the moons ever braided into our people: the speed, the surety, the fall that always lands. He had fought that whole battle already. He was burned and he was bleeding, and what he had left, he spent all at once. He went up — over the wolves, over the fire, higher than the beast's crown — and he came down spinning, frost in one hand and flame in the other, and he cut that monster in half. In half, little ones. Crown to root, one stroke, a thing three times his height. It fell in two pieces and went to black sap and dead wood, and the grove stood quiet, and underneath it all the true heart of Thornheart was still beating. It beats tonight. That is his doing.

And the city of Riften heard what he had done for it. And Riften — stone walls, shut gates, guards who ask our kind to wait outside in the cold — opened its gate. He walked through the front of it, in daylight, welcomed, while the whole market watched. A Khajiit, little ones. Through the front gate.

The wood is healing. The priestess keeps a temple in the south and prays for him still, so Ahkari has heard. And somewhere under these same two moons that made him quick, Kyboah is walking tonight — and the moons that made him made every cub at this fire.

Now. To bed."
